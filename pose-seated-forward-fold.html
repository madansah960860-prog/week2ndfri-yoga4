Pose guide

Seated Forward Fold

Paścimottānāsana

Sitting with the legs extended and folding forward from the hip joints. The pose where most people learn that the point is the hinge, not the hands.

Beginner Hold 1 to 3 minutes Hamstrings, calves, spine Strap and blanket

Pose breakdown

Paścima meaning west or the back of the body, uttāna meaning intense stretch

Entry steps

  1. Fold a blanket to about two or three inches and sit on its front edge with the legs extended forward. Sitting up on support is not optional for most people, because it lets the pelvis tip forward.
  2. Set the feet hip width apart with the toes pointing up toward the ceiling and the ankles neutral.
  3. Bend the knees. Genuinely bend them, enough that you feel the sitting bones settle down and back.
  4. Loop a strap around the balls of both feet and hold one end in each hand, arms straight.
  5. Inhale and lift the chest, drawing the lower ribs up and away from the front of the pelvis.
  6. Exhale and tip forward from the crease of the hips, leading with the breastbone rather than the forehead.
  7. Stop at the first place where the back begins to round significantly. Stay there. Bend the elbows out to the sides and let the shoulders soften.

Alignment cues

  • Hinge at the hips. The movement is the pelvis rotating forward over the thigh bones, not the spine curling.
  • Knees bent as much as needed. Bent knees with a long spine is the useful pose. Straight legs with a rounded spine is a compromise in the wrong direction.
  • Feet active but not clenched. Draw the toes back toward the shins gently. Do not grip.
  • Even sitting bones. Roll the flesh of the buttocks back and out so both sitting bones make contact with the blanket evenly.
  • Neck as part of the spine. Let the head follow the line of the back rather than craning it toward the feet.
  • Elbows wide and heavy. If you are pulling hard on the strap, you have gone past your available range. Let the arms be links, not levers.

Breath pattern

  • Nose breathing, even and unhurried.
  • Use inhales to lengthen the front of the torso a little, and exhales to soften into the fold slightly. Do not try to gain depth on every breath.
  • Four counts in, six counts out is a good working rhythm here.
  • After the first thirty seconds, stop trying to go anywhere. Let the breath do the rest of the work.

Suggested hold

  • Active version: 45 to 90 seconds.
  • Yin-style supported hold with a bolster on the legs: 3 to 5 minutes.
  • Between other poses: 30 seconds is enough to be worth doing.

Common mistakes

  • Reaching for the toes. Toe contact is a byproduct of proportions and hamstring length. It measures nothing.
  • Sitting flat on the floor. If your pelvis tips backward when you sit with legs out, the fold has to come from the spine. Sit up on a blanket.
  • Pulling with the arms. Hauling on a strap creates tension in the shoulders and neck, and the hamstrings do not respond to being yanked.
  • Locking the knees. Hyperextended knees in a forward fold puts load at the back of the knee joint rather than in the muscle.
  • Bouncing. Small pulsing movements at the end range are not helping. Settle and stay.
  • Rounding from the neck first. Dropping the head early makes the fold look deeper and makes it do less.

Accessible modifications

  • Chair version. Sit toward the front of a chair with the feet flat on the floor and the knees apart. Fold forward from the hips and rest the forearms on the thighs, or the hands on a second chair or a low table. This works the same hip hinge with no floor sitting at all.
  • Bolster on the legs. Place a bolster or a stack of two firm cushions across the thighs and rest the torso and forehead down onto it. The support means you can stay for several minutes without holding yourself up.
  • Rolled blanket under the knees. A rolled blanket under both knees takes the hamstrings out of their end range and lets the pelvis tip more freely.
  • One leg at a time. Bend one knee and place that foot flat on the floor, folding over the extended leg only. Change sides. This halves the demand.
  • Wall version. Sit with your back against a wall and simply extend the legs forward with a strap around the feet. Stay upright. This is a hamstring position without any folding at all, and it is where to begin if sitting with legs straight is already the whole challenge.
  • Higher seat. If a two-inch blanket is not enough, sit on a firm cushion or a stack of two blankets. There is no maximum height.

Progression

  • Head-to-Knee Fold. Bend one knee out to the side with the sole of the foot at the inner thigh, and fold over the straight leg. Hold 1 to 2 minutes per side. Adds a rotation and a side-body length to the same forward hinge.
  • Reduce the support gradually. Move from a three-inch blanket to two, then to one, over several months, keeping the same quality of hinge. That is progress in this pose. Touching the toes is not.
Approach with care Take extra care or choose the chair or wall version if you have a disc-related low back condition, sciatic symptoms, a hamstring strain or tear, if you have had recent abdominal or spinal surgery, or if you are pregnant, in which case take the legs wide so there is no compression at the front of the body. Come out if you feel any sharp or radiating sensation down the back of the leg, pinching in the low back, or numbness or tingling in the feet. Work with a qualified teacher for hands-on guidance. The hinge is the whole skill

If you learn to hinge at the hip joints with a long spine here, you have learned the movement that every forward fold on this site uses, from Standing Forward Fold to Pyramid to Wide-Legged Forward Fold. It transfers directly to lifting things off the floor in ordinary life.

If you learn instead to round forward and reach, you will practice that too, and it will also transfer.

This pose appears in the Gentle Full Practice and in the Evening Wind-Down in its bolster-supported form.

What to feel for

Length, not reach

Stop at the first place the back starts to round, not at the first place the hands stop moving. A long spine with bent knees is doing more than a straight-legged slump ever will. If you notice yourself hauling on the strap, that is the cue to bend the knees further and let the hips do the settling instead of the arms.
