Props are not cheating

A block under the hand does not make Triangle easier. It makes a different Triangle, and usually a better one.

Published 07/29/2026 · About 10 minutes to read

Almost everyone who starts yoga arrives with the same assumption: that props are for people who cannot do the real pose yet, and that the goal is to stop needing them. It is worth taking that assumption apart, because it leads people to practice worse versions of shapes for years.

Where the idea came from

Partly it comes from other physical activities. In most sports, assistance genuinely does indicate a stage. Training wheels come off. The pool noodle gets put away. It is reasonable to import that model into a new activity.

Partly it comes from photographs. A century of yoga imagery has featured unusually mobile bodies in unsupported shapes, and those images set an unspoken standard for what a pose looks like when it is being done correctly.

And partly it comes from the language. Calling something a modification implies a departure from an original. Calling something a prop implies propping something up that would otherwise fall.

None of these are good reasons, but together they are persuasive enough that most beginners will reach for the floor when a block is sitting right next to them.

What a prop actually does

A prop changes the geometry of a pose. That is the whole mechanism. It is not adding support to a fixed shape. It is altering the distances between the parts of your body and the floor, which changes which tissues are being loaded and in what direction.

Take Triangle. In the unsupported version, the lower hand reaches toward the floor. Whether it gets there is determined by three things that have nothing to do with skill: the length of your arms relative to your torso, the length of your legs, and how far the hip joint currently allows the pelvis to tip.

If your proportions do not put the floor within reach at the point where the spine is still long, one of two things happens. Either you stop short and hover, which means the arm is now doing isometric work it was never meant to do, or you keep going and the ribcage collapses toward the front thigh.

That second version is what most unsupported Triangles look like in a beginner class. The spine has rounded and side bent at the same time, the top shoulder has rolled forward, and the shape that was supposed to lengthen one side of the body is now compressing it.

Put a block on its tallest setting under the lower hand and the geometry resolves. The hand has somewhere to land at a height that matches your arms. The spine stays long. The chest can turn. The pose is now doing what it was described as doing.

The supported version is not the easier Triangle. It is a structurally different arrangement that happens to be the one that works for your body.

The seated blanket, the clearest example

The most useful prop in the whole practice is a folded blanket under the sitting bones, and it is the one people are most likely to skip.

Sit on the floor with your legs straight out in front of you. For a large share of adults, the pelvis immediately rolls backward, the low back rounds, and the whole torso tips behind vertical. From that position, any attempt to fold forward has to come from the spine, because the pelvis is already rotated as far back as it goes.

Sit on two or three inches of folded blanket and the pelvis comes up onto its front edge. Now the hip joints are free to rotate, and a forward fold can happen at the hips, which is what Seated Forward Fold is actually asking for.

Without the blanket, you are practicing a spinal flexion exercise and calling it a hamstring pose. With it, you are practicing the pose. The blanket did not make anything easier. It made the intended movement available.

How to choose a height

The general rule for every prop decision is the same: find the height at which the intended action is still available, and use that one.

Standing folds and side bends

The test is spinal length. Start with the block at its tallest setting and lower it one level at a time. The moment the spine starts rounding or the chest turns toward the floor, go back up a level. That is your height.

Seated shapes

The test is pelvic tilt. Sit on progressively less height until the pelvis starts rolling backward, then add one fold back.

Supported and restorative shapes

The test is whether the muscles can go completely quiet. Set it up, take your hands away, and check whether anything is still working. If it is, add height.

A strap

The test is shoulder position. If you are pulling hard enough that the shoulders lift toward the ears, lengthen the strap.

Notice that none of these tests are about how the shape looks or how far you got. They are all about whether a specific mechanical thing is still happening.

Do props ever come away?

Sometimes, and it is not the point. Over months of consistent practice, some people find they can move from a tall block to a medium one, or from three inches of blanket to two. That is a reasonable thing to notice and a reasonable thing to be pleased about.

But plenty of things that determine prop height do not change. Arm length relative to torso length does not change. Femur length does not change. The shape of your hip socket does not change. If your proportions mean that a tall block is the correct height for Triangle, that is a permanent fact about your body, not a stage you are passing through.

Experienced practitioners use blocks constantly. In an Iyengar class, everyone has an armful of props and the teacher may well have more. That is not a room full of beginners.

The chair, which is also a prop

The same argument applies at a larger scale to chair practice. A chair raises the floor by about eighteen inches and supports part of your body weight. That changes the geometry of nearly every shape, and it makes precise work possible in the joints you are actually trying to move.

Our Desk Reset is a complete chair practice. It is not a reduced version of a floor practice for people who cannot get down. It is a different practice, and for some bodies it is simply the more useful one.

What to do with this

Set the props out before you start rather than deciding mid-pose whether you deserve them. Two blocks, a strap, a blanket, within reach of the mat. Use the tallest option first and work down if and only if the test for that pose is still passing.

Every pose guide on this site lists at least two prop-supported or chair-based options, and lists them as equal alternatives rather than as fallbacks, because that is what they are.
