Props and modifications

What each prop actually does, how to set it up, and what you already own that will do the same job.

Updated 08/27/2026 · About 11 minutes to read

A prop changes the geometry of a pose so the shape meets your body where it is. A block under the hand in Triangle does not make Triangle easier. It makes a different, better version of Triangle available, one where the spine can stay long instead of collapsing toward the floor.

That is worth saying plainly, because a lot of people arrive at yoga believing props are a temporary crutch to be discarded. Teachers with twenty years of practice use blocks. The prop is part of the pose.

Everything below lists a household substitute, because none of this needs to be bought before you can practice.

Blocks

What it does

  • Raises the floor toward your hand so the spine can stay long in standing folds and side bends.
  • Supports the pelvis in seated shapes and the thighs in hip-opening shapes.
  • Gives the head somewhere to rest in kneeling and seated folds.
  • Sits under the pelvis as the entire structure of Supported Bridge.

How to choose and set up

  • Standard dimensions are 9 by 6 by 4 inches, giving three heights depending on which face you rest on.
  • Cork is heavy and firm and does not slide. Foam is light and softer and better under the head or hips.
  • Two blocks are far more useful than one. Almost every use case wants a matched pair.
  • Start on the tallest setting in every standing pose. Lower it only when the spine can stay long.

Household substitute

  • Two thick hardcover books of equal thickness, taped shut so they cannot slide open.
  • A stack of two or three firmly folded bath towels for anything where you rest rather than push.
  • A shoebox packed tightly with folded clothes so it will not crush.

Used on this site in

  • Supported Bridge, Downward-Facing Dog, Triangle, Standing Forward Fold and Bound Angle.

Strap

What it does

  • Extends your reach so you can hold a foot without rounding the spine to get there.
  • Binds the thighs or feet together so muscles can stop working to hold a position.
  • Links the hands behind the back in shoulder shapes where they do not meet.

How to choose and set up

  • Six feet is enough for most people. Eight feet is more useful if you are over six feet tall or want to loop the whole body.
  • A D-ring or cinch buckle lets you make a fixed loop, which is what you want for binding the thighs.
  • Hold the strap with straight arms and bent elbows rather than pulling. It is a link, not a winch.

Household substitute

  • A bathrobe belt. This is the closest free equivalent and it works perfectly.
  • A long scarf, a necktie, or a dog leash with the clip end held in your hand.
  • A bath towel held at both ends, for shoulder work.

Used on this site in

  • Seated Forward Fold, Head-to-Knee Fold, Cow Face Arms and Happy Baby.

Bolster

What it does

  • Takes the weight of the torso in supported shapes so the muscles can go completely quiet.
  • Sits under the knees, the pelvis or the length of the spine depending on the pose.
  • Turns a two minute shape into a ten minute one, because you are no longer holding yourself up.

How to choose and set up

  • Rectangular bolsters are about 25 by 12 by 6 inches and are the most versatile. Round bolsters give a higher arch and are better for chest opening.
  • Firmness matters more than size. A bolster that flattens under you is doing nothing.
  • When in doubt, add a folded blanket on top rather than buying a taller bolster.

Household substitute

  • Two firm bed pillows placed end to end inside one pillowcase so they stay together.
  • A couch cushion, which is usually firmer than a bed pillow.
  • A comforter rolled tightly and tied with two belts.

Used on this site in

  • The whole Evening Wind-Down, and the supported versions of Child's Pose and Seated Forward Fold.

Blankets

What it does

  • Pads the knees, ankles, hips and spine against a hard floor.
  • Raises the pelvis in seated shapes so it can tip forward, which is the single most useful adjustment in the whole practice.
  • Supports the neck under the shoulders in Supported Bridge.
  • Covers the body during long stillness, when temperature drops.

How to choose and set up

  • A firm woven blanket that holds a fold is what you want. Fleece and knit blankets slump and are less useful.
  • Learn two folds: the long fold, which gives a firm narrow strip for under the spine, and the square fold, which gives a firm seat.
  • Two blankets is the practical minimum. Three is comfortable.

Household substitute

  • Any firmly woven blanket you already own. A wool camp blanket is ideal.
  • Two or three bath towels folded together.

The chair

What it does

  • Supports part of your body weight so you can work precisely in the joints you actually want to move.
  • Raises the floor by around 18 inches, which changes almost every standing and seated shape.
  • Makes an entire practice available without getting down to or up from the floor.

How to choose and set up

  • A dining chair with a flat seat and no arms. Wood or metal rather than upholstered.
  • Set the back legs against a wall so the chair cannot slide when you lean on it.
  • If your feet do not reach the floor, put a book or a folded jacket under them.
  • If your hips sit lower than your knees, sit on a folded towel so the hips are slightly higher.

What it replaces

  • Child's Pose becomes a seated fold over cushions on the thighs.
  • Downward-Facing Dog becomes hands on the chair seat with the feet walked back.
  • Warrior II becomes a seated straddle with one thigh along the seat.
  • Legs Up the Wall becomes calves resting on the chair seat, which many people find more comfortable than the wall version.
  • Supported Bridge becomes a gentle chest opening over the chair back.

Used on this site in

  • The whole Desk Reset, and the beginner path of every other sequence.

The wall

What it does

  • Gives you a flat vertical reference, which tells you immediately when your torso is leaning without you noticing.
  • Removes the balance element from balance poses so you can work on the shape itself.
  • Holds your legs up in Legs Up the Wall.
  • Provides a surface for the hands in Wall Dog, which is the best way to learn Downward-Facing Dog.

How to set up

  • You want a clear stretch of wall with no baseboard heater, no picture rail at shoulder height, and nothing hanging on it.
  • For hand contact, mark the hip-height point mentally so you return to the same place each time.
  • For Legs Up the Wall, put a folded blanket on the floor where your torso will lie before you swivel in.

Used on this site in

  • Legs Up the Wall, Wall Dog in Downward-Facing Dog, Tree Pose, Half Moon and Warrior III.

The mat

What it does

  • Stops the hands and feet sliding, which is its actual job.
  • Provides a small amount of padding, though not enough for kneeling on a hard floor.
  • Defines a space, which matters more psychologically than it sounds.

How to choose

  • Standard size is 68 by 24 inches. Longer mats at 72 or 74 inches suit people over about 5 feet 10 inches.
  • Thickness between 4 and 6 millimeters is the usual range. Thicker mats are softer underfoot but less stable in standing poses.
  • Grip matters far more than brand or pattern. If your hands slide forward in Downward-Facing Dog, the mat is wrong for you.

Household substitute

  • A large bath towel over carpet, for floor-only practice.
  • A rug with a non-slip underlay.
  • Bare carpet works for everything except standing poses, where feet sink and the base becomes unstable.

What to buy first, if you buy anything

In order of how much difference each one makes to a home practice.

  1. A mat

    The only item with no good household substitute for standing work.

  2. Two blocks

    Used in more poses than anything else on this list.

  3. Two firm blankets

    If you already own a wool blanket, you own this one.

  4. A strap

    Skip it if you already have a bathrobe belt.

  5. A bolster

    The most expensive item and the last one to need. Two pillows will carry you for months.

A chair and a wall cost nothing and you already have both. Between them they cover a complete practice.
