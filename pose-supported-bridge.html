Supported Bridge

Setu Bandha Sarvāṅgāsana

Lying on the back with the pelvis resting on a block. A backward bend in which a prop does the holding, so the shape can be kept for minutes rather than breaths.

Beginner Hold 2 to 5 minutes Front of hips, chest, spine One block

Pose breakdown

Setu meaning bridge, bandha meaning lock or bind, sarvāṅga meaning all limbs

Entry steps

  1. Lie on your back on a mat with the knees bent and the feet flat on the floor, hip width apart and parallel.
  2. Walk the heels in until the fingertips can just graze them. This is the distance that lets the shins stay close to vertical when the hips lift.
  3. Have a block within reach on its lowest setting for a first attempt.
  4. Press evenly through both feet and lift the hips a few inches off the floor. Keep the movement small.
  5. Slide the block under the bony back of the pelvis, the flat plate of bone above the tailbone. It should not sit under the low back, and it should not sit under the tailbone itself.
  6. Lower the pelvis onto the block and let the weight settle. Take your hands away and let the arms rest alongside the body.
  7. To come out, press through the feet, lift the hips, remove the block with one hand and lower the spine to the floor one section at a time.

Alignment cues

  • Block under bone, not under soft tissue. If the low back is on the block, the block is too high on the body. Slide it toward the tailbone.
  • Feet parallel and hip width. Feet that splay outward turn the thighs out and change what the front of the hips is being asked to do.
  • Knees pointing forward. Let them stay in line with the second toe rather than falling in or out.
  • Neck long, chin slightly away from the chest. A folded blanket under the shoulders keeps the neck from flattening.
  • Shoulders drawing down toward the hips. Roll the outer shoulders under a little so the chest can broaden.
  • No gripping in the buttocks. Once the block is in place, the muscles should be able to release entirely. If they cannot, the block is too tall.

Breath pattern

  • Breathe in and out through the nose, quietly.
  • The chest is open here, so let the inhale be unforced and full rather than pushed.
  • Lengthen the exhale gradually toward twice the length of the inhale over the course of the hold, for example four counts in and eight counts out, but only if it stays comfortable.
  • If the breath feels restricted at the throat, lower the block one setting.

Suggested hold

  • First time: 60 to 90 seconds on the lowest block setting.
  • Regular practice: 2 to 3 minutes.
  • As a long restorative hold: 5 minutes with a bolster instead of a block.
  • Come out slowly, then lie flat for at least 30 seconds before doing anything else.

Common mistakes

  • Block too tall too soon. The tallest setting is a considerable arch. Start low and stay there for several weeks.
  • Block placed under the low back. This concentrates the curve into a small section of the spine. Move it down onto the flat of the pelvis.
  • Feet too far from the hips. The hips end up hanging rather than resting and the hamstrings do work they do not need to do.
  • Pushing the chest up. This is a supported shape. Once the block is in, stop working.
  • Coming out fast. Rolling off the block in one movement is where low backs get grumpy. Lift, remove, lower.
  • Turning the head during the hold. Keep the head still and facing up while the chest is open.

Accessible modifications

  • Bolster instead of a block. A bolster laid across the mat under the pelvis is softer, lower and easier to stay on for five minutes. Two firmly folded blankets work identically.
  • Folded blanket under the shoulders. Place a blanket folded to about one inch under the shoulders and upper back with the head off the edge on the floor. This protects the neck from flattening and is worth doing every time.
  • Chair version. Sit on the front edge of a chair, hold the sides of the seat, slide the hips forward slightly and let the upper back curve gently over the chair back with the head supported by a cushion on the backrest. This gives a supported chest opening with no floor work at all.
  • Wall version. Lie with the feet flat on a wall rather than the floor, knees bent at roughly ninety degrees. The wall makes it easy to keep the shins vertical and the pressure even.
  • Strap around the thighs. Loop a strap around the thighs just above the knees at hip width so the legs can rest against it instead of being actively held.
  • Blanket roll under the feet. If the heels feel strained, place a folded blanket under them so the ankle angle is less extreme.

Progression

  • Active Bridge. Remove the block and lift the hips using the legs, holding for 20 to 30 seconds, three rounds. This turns a supported shape into muscular work for the back of the legs and hips.
  • One-legged Supported Bridge. With the block in place and stable, extend one leg along the floor for 30 seconds, then change. Keep the pelvis level, which is harder than it sounds.
Approach with care Take extra care or choose the chair version if you have a neck injury or have been advised to avoid neck flexion, if you have a low back condition that is irritated by extension, if you have had recent spinal, abdominal or shoulder surgery, if you have uncontrolled high blood pressure, or if you experience reflux when lying with the chest above the head. From the second trimester of pregnancy onward, lying flat on the back for long periods is generally not advised, so use a chair or side-lying alternative. Stop if you feel low back pinching, neck pressure, headache or shortness of breath. Work with a qualified teacher for hands-on guidance. See the full Health & Safety Disclaimer.

Choosing the right height

A cork or foam block has three heights. Most people should spend their first month on the lowest, and a good number of people will never need more than the middle one. The test is simple. Once you are on the block and your hands are off, can the muscles of your hips and legs go completely quiet? If yes, the height is right. If you have to keep working to stay there, come down a level.

What to feel for

Nothing left to hold

Once the block is set and you take your hands away, the hips and legs should be able to go fully quiet. That is the test. If you are still working to stay up, the block is too tall — come down a setting rather than pushing through it. A short practice note: this is one to return to on days when nothing else sounds appealing. The block does the work.
