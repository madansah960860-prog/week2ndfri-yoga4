Beginner's guide

Start here, tonight.

Everything you need to take a first practice in the room you already have, with the clothes you already own.

10-Minute Morning Evening Wind-Down
8-minute read4 starter posesNo equipment required

Most beginner yoga advice assumes a studio, a teacher and ninety free minutes. This guide assumes none of that. It assumes you have a floor, roughly six and a half feet by three feet of clear space, and somewhere between ten and twenty minutes.

Read this page once from top to bottom. It takes about eight minutes. Then go do the 10-Minute Morning sequence or the Evening Wind-Down, whichever is closer to the time of day you are reading this.

Before you begin Aruna Yoga Method publishes general movement education. It is not medical advice, and nothing here diagnoses, treats or prevents any condition. Talk with a qualified healthcare professional before starting any new physical practice, particularly if you are pregnant, recently injured, post-surgical or managing a health condition. If something hurts, stop. Read the full Health & Safety Disclaimer.

1. What you actually need

Very little. In order of how much difference it makes:

  • A non-slip surface. A mat is the single most useful item because it stops your hands and feet sliding. A folded bath towel on carpet works for floor-only practice. Hardwood without a mat is genuinely slippery, so if that is your floor, keep your first practices seated and lying down until you have a mat.
  • Clothes you can bend in. Anything that does not restrict your hips or dig in at the waist. Fitted enough that a shirt does not fall over your face when you tip forward. Bare feet, or grip socks if your floor is cold.
  • Two firm supports. Blocks are ideal. Two thick hardcover books taped together, or a stack of two folded bath towels, do the same job for a while.
  • A strap. A bathrobe belt, a long scarf or a dog leash all work. You use it to reach your foot when your hands do not get there.
  • A blanket. For under the knees, under the hips, and over you at the end. A folded comforter is perfect.

That is the whole list. Read Props & Modifications for what each item does and how to substitute from around the house.

2. How much space

Lie on your back with your arms out to the sides. If nothing hits a wall or furniture, you have enough for almost everything on this site. In numbers, a standard mat is 68 inches by 24 inches, and you want roughly 12 inches of clearance on each long side so your arms can open.

If you cannot get that, you can still practice. A chair-based practice such as Desk Reset needs about three feet by three feet. Wall poses such as Legs Up the Wall need a clear stretch of wall and the width of your body.

3. When to practice

The honest answer is whenever you will actually do it. There is no physiologically correct hour. There are practical trade-offs.

  • Morning. The body is stiffer after a night in bed, so joints move through a smaller range at first. Warm up longer and expect less depth. The advantage is that nothing has been added to the day yet to push practice aside.
  • Midday. The most mobile part of the day for most people. Good for standing shapes and anything that asks for balance.
  • Evening. Best for long supported holds and floor work, once the day is mostly behind you and nothing else is competing for your attention. Use the hold timer and stay in shapes longer.

Leave roughly two hours after a large meal before folding forward or twisting. A snack is fine.

4. Your first four poses

Learn these four before anything else. They cover the four directions the spine moves, they can all be modified, and every sequence on this site uses at least two of them.

  1. Child's Pose

    The resting shape you return to whenever you need a break.

  2. Seated Forward Fold

    Where you learn what a hamstring stretch actually feels like and how to stop chasing your toes.

  3. Supported Bridge

    A gentle backward bend that a block makes easy.

  4. Legs Up the Wall

    The one to finish with.

When those feel familiar, add Downward-Facing Dog and Warrior II.

5. How to breathe while you practice

For your first month, one rule is enough: breathe in and out through your nose, and do not hold your breath. If you notice you are holding it, you have gone deeper into a shape than your body is currently willing to go. Back off until the breath returns on its own.

Once that is automatic, the next step is lengthening the exhale so it lasts longer than the inhale. Our Breathwork Basics page has a visual pacer you can breathe along with and clear notes on when to stop.

6. How long to stay in a pose

It depends entirely on the style of shape:

  • Standing shapes that ask for muscular work: three to eight breaths, roughly 20 to 45 seconds.
  • Gentle floor shapes: five to ten breaths, roughly 45 to 90 seconds.
  • Supported and Yin-style shapes with a prop holding your weight: two to five minutes.

Use the hold timer in the corner of this page rather than counting. It quietly dims the page while a hold runs, so the screen stops pulling at your attention — a small focus cue, not a mood setting — and everything returns to full brightness the moment the hold ends. Every pose page on this site lists a suggested hold you can send straight to the timer.

7. What a first practice should feel like

Broad, spread-out sensation in the middle of a muscle is what you are looking for. It should be something you could hold a conversation through. Sharp, pinching, electrical, burning or joint-line sensation is not, and it is the signal to come out and set the shape up differently.

You may feel warm. You may find your legs shake slightly in a standing shape, which is ordinary muscular fatigue. You may feel more aware of one side than the other. Bodies are asymmetric. That is normal and not a problem to fix in week one.

8. What to skip at the start

  • Headstand, handstand and shoulderstand. These need a teacher watching you, not a web page.
  • Deep backward bending such as full Wheel. There is no rush and no benefit to arriving early.
  • Hot rooms. Heat makes a shape feel more available than it structurally is, which is the exact condition in which people overshoot.
  • Anyone adjusting your body without asking. In a class, you are always allowed to say no.

9. A realistic first month

  1. Week one

    Three sessions of ten minutes. Use the 10-Minute Morning sequence each time. Repetition is the point.

  2. Week two

    Four sessions. Keep three short and add one twenty minute Evening Wind-Down.

  3. Week three

    Add the Desk Reset on a work day, whenever your shoulders start climbing toward your ears.

  4. Week four

    One Gentle Full Practice at forty minutes, plus your short sessions. If forty feels long, do the first half and finish with Legs Up the Wall.

Missing a day is not a failure state. The only thing that matters is that the next session happens.

10. When to work with a teacher

A written guide can describe a shape well. It cannot see you. Work with a qualified teacher in person if you are returning from an injury or surgery, if you are pregnant or recently postpartum, if a shape consistently produces sensation you cannot place, or simply if you want hands-on feedback on the four poses above. Our Find a Teacher directory is a starting point for looking locally.
