Breathwork basics

Four patterns, what each one asks you to do, how long to practice it, and the signals that mean stop. Plus a pacer you can breathe along with.

Updated 08/18/2026 · About 9 minutes to read

Breathing practices in yoga are usually grouped under the Sanskrit word prāṇāyāma. In a modern class it means deliberately changing the length, path or rhythm of the breath for a set period of time, then returning to ordinary breathing.

This page describes what each pattern involves and how to practice it. It does not describe what any of them do to your body, because that is a medical question and this site does not answer medical questions. If you are curious about physiological effects, that is a conversation for a qualified healthcare professional.

Read this before you practice any pattern below Breath practices are general movement education here, not medical advice, and nothing on this page treats or prevents any condition. Talk with a qualified healthcare professional before beginning breath practice, particularly if you are pregnant, have a respiratory condition such as asthma, have a cardiovascular condition, have low or high blood pressure, are prone to fainting, have a history of panic or dissociation, or have had recent surgery. Stop immediately and return to normal breathing if you feel lightheaded, dizzy, tingling in the hands or face, chest tightness, visual changes, nausea or anxiety. Never practice breath holds in water or while driving. Read the full Health & Safety Disclaimer.

The pacer

The ring below expands as you breathe in and contracts as you breathe out, at four counts in and six counts out. Follow it with your eyes or, better, glance at it once and then close your eyes and keep the rhythm yourself.

Paused

Four counts in, six counts out. If six counts out feels like a strain, breathe out for five, or four. The pattern should never feel like effort.

1. Nose breathing

The default for every practice on this site

How to practice it

  1. Sit or lie in a position where the spine is long and you are not holding yourself up. Sitting up on a folded blanket helps.
  2. Close the mouth. Breathe in and out through the nose only.
  3. Do not lengthen or shorten anything yet. Simply notice the pace your breath is already at.
  4. Continue for two minutes.

What to look for

  • Movement at the lower ribs and belly rather than only at the top of the chest.
  • An even sound, or no sound at all.
  • No pause of effort at the top of the inhale.

When to stop

  • If your nose is congested and nose breathing is a struggle, breathe through the mouth. There is no benefit in fighting it.
  • If you find yourself gasping at the end of an exhale, the pace has been forced. Return to normal breathing.

2. Extended exhale

Sometimes taught as 1:2 breathing

How to practice it

  1. Start with the pacer above, or count silently at a comfortable speed.
  2. Breathe in for four counts.
  3. Breathe out for six counts.
  4. Repeat for ten rounds, then return to ordinary breathing for one minute.
  5. Over weeks, you may find eight counts out becomes available. There is no requirement to get there.

Common mistakes

  • Forcing the count. If you are squeezing air out at the end, shorten the exhale by one or two counts.
  • Making the inhale huge. The inhale stays ordinary. Only the exhale changes.
  • Practicing too long. Ten to twenty rounds is plenty. This is not an endurance event.

When to stop

  • Lightheadedness, tingling in the fingers or face, or any sense of air hunger.
  • If counting itself makes you anxious, drop the counting and simply make each exhale a little longer than each inhale.

3. Box breathing

Sama Vṛtti, meaning equal movement

How to practice it

  1. Breathe in for four counts.
  2. Hold with the lungs comfortably full for four counts. The throat stays open and the face stays soft.
  3. Breathe out for four counts.
  4. Hold with the lungs comfortably empty for four counts.
  5. That is one round. Practice six to ten rounds, no more, then return to ordinary breathing.

Important notes on the holds

  • The holds are pauses, not clamps. If you are gripping the throat or the belly, shorten them to two counts.
  • Start with a three count square before you try four.
  • This is the pattern most likely to cause dizziness in people new to breath practice, because of the holds.

When to skip this one entirely

  • During pregnancy, breath holds are generally not recommended. Use the extended exhale instead.
  • If you have a cardiovascular or respiratory condition, or high or low blood pressure, do not practice breath holds without guidance from a qualified healthcare professional.
  • If you have a history of panic, holds can be uncomfortable. There is no obligation to practice this pattern.

4. Alternate nostril breathing

Nāḍī Śodhana

How to practice it

  1. Sit comfortably with the spine long. Rest the left hand on the knee.
  2. Bring the right thumb to the right nostril and the right ring finger to the left nostril, with the index and middle fingers folded in or resting on the forehead.
  3. Close the right nostril with the thumb. Breathe out, then in, through the left nostril for four counts.
  4. Close the left nostril with the ring finger, release the thumb, and breathe out through the right for four to six counts.
  5. Breathe in through the right for four counts. Close the right, release the left, and breathe out through the left.
  6. That is one round. Practice five to ten rounds, always finishing with an exhale through the left, then lower the hand and breathe normally.

Common mistakes

  • Pressing hard on the nose. The touch is light. You are closing a nostril, not compressing your face.
  • Lifting the elbow. Keep the right elbow low and the shoulder relaxed, or support the elbow with your other hand.
  • Continuing when congested. If one side is blocked, skip this pattern today.

When to stop

  • Any dizziness, any strain, or any sense that you are working to get air.
  • If your arm gets tired, lower it. Fatigue in the shoulder defeats the purpose.

How to build breath into a practice

  • Before poses. Two minutes of plain nose breathing, sitting or lying. This is what the first step of the Gentle Full Practice is for.
  • During poses. Nose breathing at whatever pace the shape allows. Do not try to run a pattern while holding a demanding pose.
  • After poses. Ten rounds of extended exhale while lying down, before the final rest.
  • On its own. Five minutes seated, once a day, is a complete practice. It does not need to be attached to anything.
