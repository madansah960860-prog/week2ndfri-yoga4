This page covers how to choose a teacher, what to ask, and how our directory works.

Free standard listings Directory opens this fall Five questions worth asking first

Why work with a teacher at all

Everything on this site is written to be practiced from. What it cannot do is watch your knee drift inward in Warrior II, or notice that you are holding your breath at the bottom of a fold, or tell you that the sensation you are describing is in a place that means you should stop.

Working in person is particularly worth it if you are returning from an injury or surgery, if you are pregnant or recently postpartum, if a shape consistently produces sensation you cannot place, or if you simply want feedback on the four foundational poses in our beginner's guide.

What to look for

  • A qualification you can verify. Ask what training the teacher completed, with whom, and how many hours. Most reputable teachers will tell you immediately.
  • Continuing education. Ask what they have studied since their initial training. A teacher who last trained a decade ago and has done nothing since is a different proposition from one who studies every year.
  • Specific experience with your situation. Prenatal, postnatal, chair-based, larger bodies, older adults, returning from injury. These are distinct skill sets and not every teacher has them.
  • A consent policy for hands-on adjustment. A good teacher asks before touching you and offers a way to opt out at the start of class. This is now standard practice at well-run studios.
  • Willingness to offer alternatives. Listen for whether they give a chair or wall option as an equal choice or as a consolation prize.

Questions worth asking before your first class

  • What is the pace of this class, and how much of the hour is spent moving?
  • Are props provided, or should I bring my own?
  • What is your policy on hands-on adjustments?
  • I am new. Will there be modifications offered, or should I take a different class first?
  • Is there anything you would want to know about my body before I come?

Any teacher who is irritated by these questions has answered the most important one.

A note on what a teacher is and is not A yoga teacher is a movement educator. A yoga teacher is not a doctor, physical therapist, chiropractor or any other clinical professional, unless they separately hold that qualification. If a teacher offers to diagnose or treat a condition, that is a reason to look elsewhere. Aruna Yoga Method does not verify the credentials of any teacher listed and does not endorse any individual. The directory

The listing format below shows exactly what a directory entry contains. Live listings open in the fall.

Sample listing format

Teacher name

City, State

  • Featured placement
  • Chair and prop focused
  • In person and online

Teaches

  • Styles offered, class levels, and whether group, private or both.

Training

  • Qualification, awarding body and year, exactly as the teacher supplies it. We publish it as given and we do not verify it.

Contact

  • Website, email and phone, supplied by the teacher.

Sample listing format

Studio name

City, State

  • Free listing
  • Beginner classes
  • Accessible entrance

Class types

  • The styles on the timetable, and which of them are labeled for beginners.

Access notes

  • Step-free entrance, chair-based classes offered, parking, and whether props are provided.

Contact

  • Website, email, phone and street address, supplied by the studio.
