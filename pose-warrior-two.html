Warrior II

Vīrabhadrāsana II

A wide standing shape with the front knee bent, the pelvis turned toward the long edge of the mat and the arms level. Sustained work for the thighs and hips.

Beginner Hold 30 to 45 seconds per side Hips, thighs, shoulders No prop needed

Pose breakdown

Vīrabhadra, a figure from Indian mythology, and āsana meaning posture

Entry steps

  1. Stand facing one long edge of the mat and step the feet three and a half to four feet apart. Taller bodies want the wider end of that range.
  2. Turn the right foot out to ninety degrees so the toes point along the mat.
  3. Turn the left toes in about fifteen degrees. The back foot is not parallel to the mat edge.
  4. Line up the right heel with the arch of the left foot. Widen the feet slightly apart from front to back if balance feels uncertain.
  5. Bend the right knee toward ninety degrees, aiming the kneecap over the second and third toes. Go only as far as your thigh strength will hold.
  6. Reach the arms out to shoulder height with the palms facing the floor, shoulders down away from the ears.
  7. Turn the head to look past the right hand, or keep looking forward if the neck prefers that.

Alignment cues

  • The torso stays upright. The most common drift is leaning toward the front leg. Stack the ribs directly over the pelvis.
  • Front knee tracks outward. Press the front thigh gently open so the knee does not fall toward the midline.
  • Back leg is active. Press through the outer edge of the back foot and keep the back leg straight but not locked.
  • Pelvis faces the long edge. Draw the front hip crease back and the back hip forward so the pelvis turns toward the side, not toward the front foot.
  • Arms level and long. Reach from the center of the chest out through both hands equally. The front arm tends to get more attention than the back one.
  • Front knee over the ankle. If the knee travels past the ankle, the stance is too short. Step the back foot further away.

Breath pattern

  • Steady nasal breathing, roughly four counts in and four counts out.
  • Set the shape on an exhale, then let the breath find its own rhythm rather than pacing it.
  • Shortening breath is the reliable signal that the hold has gone on long enough. Come up, shake the legs out, and change sides.

Suggested hold

  • First month: 20 to 30 seconds per side, two rounds.
  • Regular practice: 30 to 45 seconds per side.
  • Building leg endurance: 60 seconds per side, one round, with the front knee bent less deeply.

Common mistakes

  • Stance too narrow. Almost everyone stands too short. A short stance forces the knee past the ankle and puts the load in the wrong place.
  • Front knee dropping inward. Watch the kneecap. If it drifts toward the big toe, the outer hip is not participating.
  • Leaning the torso forward. The chest should be over the pelvis, not over the front thigh.
  • Shoulders climbing. Reaching the arms hard tends to lift the shoulders. Reach wide, not up.
  • Back foot lifting at the outer edge. Keep the outer edge of the back foot in contact with the floor throughout.
  • Bending the front knee deeper than the thigh can hold. A shallower bend held well is worth more than a deep one that collapses.

Accessible modifications

  • Chair version. Sit sideways on a sturdy chair with the right thigh supported along the seat, the right knee bent at ninety degrees over the front edge, and the left leg extended out to the side with the foot turned slightly in. Open the arms to shoulder height. This gives the whole upper body and hip action with the chair carrying the leg work.
  • Wall support. Practice with your back against a wall so the back of the head, upper back and tailbone lightly touch it. The wall shows you immediately when the torso starts leaning forward.
  • Shorter, higher stance. Take the feet closer together and bend the front knee only 30 or 40 degrees. This is the sustainable version if the thighs fatigue quickly or if you are managing knee sensitivity.
  • Hand on a chair back. Stand alongside a chair and rest the front hand lightly on its back for balance while the other arm reaches out.
  • Arms lower. Rest the hands on the hips instead of extending them if holding the arms at shoulder height is the limiting factor. The legs are still doing the same work.
  • Back heel on a folded blanket. If the back heel does not settle to the floor, raise the floor to meet it with a folded blanket under the outer heel.

Progression

  • Extended Side Angle. From a steady Warrior II, lower the front forearm onto the front thigh and reach the top arm over the ear. Hold 30 seconds per side. Same legs, added length through one side of the body.
  • Reverse Warrior. Turn the front palm up and lift that arm overhead while the back hand slides down the back leg. Hold 15 to 20 seconds per side, keeping the front knee bent the whole time.
Approach with care Take extra care or use the chair version if you have a knee, hip or ankle injury, if you have had a hip or knee replacement, if you have a shoulder condition that makes holding the arms out painful, if you experience dizziness on standing, or if balance is currently unreliable. During pregnancy the wide stance is generally comfortable but avoid deep knee bends that leave you unable to breathe evenly. Stop if you feel knee-line pain, groin pinching or shoulder pain. Work with a qualified teacher for hands-on guidance. See the full Health & Safety Disclaimer.

Why this pose gets rushed

Warrior II looks like a shape you either can or cannot do, so people set it up in two seconds and hold it for five. Almost all of its value is in the setup. Foot placement, stance width and pelvic orientation take fifteen seconds to get right and change entirely what the legs and hips are being asked to do.

Set it up slowly. Then hold it for less time than you think, and hold it well.

It appears in the Gentle Full Practice, held for 40 seconds per side, with the chair version written into the beginner path.

What to feel for

A steady burn, not a strain

The front thigh should feel like sustained work, not a sharp pull anywhere near the knee. Check the torso: if it has drifted over the front leg, stack the ribs back over the pelvis and the legs will do less arguing. A stance that is wide enough almost always feels more stable than one that looks tidy.
