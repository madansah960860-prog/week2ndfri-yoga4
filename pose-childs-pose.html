Pose guide

Child's Pose

Bālāsana

A kneeling forward fold that lengthens the muscles along the back of the spine and lets the head rest below the heart. The shape to return to whenever a practice asks for more than you have.

Beginner Hold 1 to 3 minutes Spine, hips, shoulders Blanket optional

Pose breakdown

Bālāsana, from bāla meaning child and āsana meaning seat or posture

Entry steps

  1. Kneel on a mat with the shins parallel behind you and the big toes lightly touching.
  2. Take the knees as wide as the outer edges of the mat. Keeping them together is the narrower variation and it is equally valid.
  3. Draw the hips back toward the heels. If they do not reach, that is expected and there is nothing to force.
  4. Walk both hands forward along the floor until the torso lowers between or over the thighs.
  5. Bring the forehead to the mat, to stacked fists or to a block, whichever surface arrives first without straining the neck.
  6. Let the arms extend forward with soft elbows, or slide them back alongside the body with the palms turned up.

Alignment cues

  • Weight in the hips, not the head. The forehead rests, it does not press. If the neck is loading, the head support is too low.
  • Length before depth. Reach the tailbone back and the crown forward at the same time, so the spine lengthens rather than compressing into a heap.
  • Shoulders wide. Let the shoulder blades spread apart across the back rather than squeezing toward each other.
  • Jaw and tongue soft. Unclench the back teeth. This is the quickest way to tell whether you are resting or performing.
  • Tops of the feet down. Let the ankles lengthen along the floor. Tuck a rolled towel under them if that position pinches.

Breath pattern

  • Breathe in and out through the nose at an even, unhurried pace.
  • Direct the inhale into the back and sides of the ribcage. The front of the body is pressed against the thighs, so the back of the ribs is where the movement is available.
  • Aim for an exhale that is slightly longer than the inhale, roughly four counts in and six counts out, without straining to get there.
  • Count six to twelve full rounds if you would rather not watch a clock.

Suggested hold

  • As a rest between shapes: 20 to 45 seconds, or three to five breaths.
  • As a pose in its own right: 1 to 3 minutes.
  • As a supported Yin-style hold with a bolster under the torso: 3 to 5 minutes.

Common mistakes

  • Forcing the hips to the heels. If there is a gap, fill it with a folded blanket rather than dragging the hips down.
  • Hovering the head. Holding the head an inch off the floor keeps the neck working for the entire hold. Give it something to land on.
  • Gripping the arms. Straight, actively reaching arms turn a resting shape into a shoulder exercise. Soften the elbows or take the arms back.
  • Knees too wide too soon. Very wide knees can pinch at the front of the hip. Narrow them until the fold feels clean.
  • Holding the breath at the bottom. Breath holding means the position is compressing you somewhere. Add height under the torso.

Accessible modifications

  • Blanket behind the knees. Roll a blanket to about two inches thick and place it behind the knee joints before you sit back. This reduces how far the knees have to bend and is the first thing to try if the knees complain.
  • Blanket or block under the hips. Sit on a folded blanket between the hips and heels so the pelvis is supported at the height it actually reaches today.
  • Bolster under the torso. Place a bolster, or two firm pillows end to end, lengthwise between the thighs and rest the whole front of the body along it. Turn the head to one side, and switch sides halfway through.
  • Block under the forehead. Stack a block at any of its three heights under the forehead so the neck stays long.
  • Chair version. Sit toward the front of a chair with the feet flat and the knees apart. Fold forward and rest the forearms and forehead on a second chair, a table or a stack of cushions on your thighs. This gives the same long back body without any load through the knees or ankles.
  • Standing wall version. Stand a forearm's length from a wall, place the forearms on the wall at shoulder height and walk the feet back until the torso is roughly horizontal. Rest the forehead on the forearms. Useful when getting to the floor is not practical.

Progression

  • Thread the Needle. From the wide-knee version, slide the right arm under the body and rest on the right shoulder and the side of the head. Hold 45 to 90 seconds, then change sides. This takes the same base and adds rotation through the upper spine.
  • Extended side reach. With the hands forward, walk both hands to the left and hold for 30 to 60 seconds, then walk them to the right. This lengthens along one side of the ribcage at a time.
Approach with care Take extra care or choose the chair version if you have a knee or ankle injury, if you are pregnant (widen the knees generously so there is no pressure on the abdomen, and use a bolster), if you have had recent abdominal or hip surgery, if deep knee flexion is uncomfortable, or if putting the head below the heart is something a healthcare professional has asked you to avoid. Come out if you feel pinching at the front of the hip or the knee joint, tingling in the feet, or pressure in the head or eyes. Work with a qualified teacher for hands-on guidance rather than relying on a written description alone. See the full Health & Safety Disclaimer.

Where it fits in a practice

Child's Pose does three jobs. It is a warm-up at the start of a floor practice, where a minute here tells you what your hips and knees are willing to do today. It is a reset in the middle of a practice, taken whenever you need it and without asking anyone. And it is a closing shape before lying down, particularly after backward bending.

It appears in the Evening Wind-Down and the Gentle Full Practice. In the evening sequence it is held with a bolster for three minutes, which turns it into a supported shape rather than an active one.

What to feel for

Weight settling, not pressing

The forehead should rest, never press. If the neck is doing anything at all, the support underneath it is too low — add a block or stacked fists before you add time. What you are looking for is the low back and the backs of the ribs opening on every inhale, and the jaw going slack somewhere in the first minute. That release is the whole pose.
