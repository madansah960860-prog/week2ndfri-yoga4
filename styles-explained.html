Styles explained

Six words that appear on studio timetables, what each one actually means in the room, and how to pick the one that suits what you want from an hour.

Updated 08/22/2026 · About 12 minutes to read

Style names on a schedule are a rough signal, not a guarantee. Two teachers can both call a class Hatha and run it very differently. What the labels do reliably tell you is the pace, how long you will stay in each shape, and how much of the hour is spent moving versus resting.

Below is what each of the six most common labels usually means in a studio in the United States, what a typical class looks like from the inside, and which of our sequences is closest to it if you want to try the shape of that practice at home first.

Hatha

Haṭha, often translated as force or effort

What it is

  • In modern studio use, Hatha is the general-purpose label for a class of individual poses held for several breaths, with pauses between them, at a moderate pace.
  • Historically the term covers a much wider body of practice than physical postures, including breathing and cleansing techniques. On a timetable it almost always means the slower, pose-by-pose format.

What an hour looks like

  • Five to ten minutes of seated or lying warm-up and breathing.
  • Thirty to forty minutes of standing and seated shapes, each held for five to eight breaths with instruction between them.
  • Five to ten minutes of floor shapes and a final rest.

Who tends to like it

  • People who want to hear how a pose is built rather than being carried through it.
  • Anyone new to yoga. The pauses give you time to correct yourself.
  • People who find fast classes leave them unclear about what they just did.

Closest sequence here

  • Gentle Full Practice, which follows the same warm-up, standing, seated, rest arc.

Vinyasa

Vinyāsa, meaning to place in a particular way

What it is

  • Poses linked into continuous sequences, with one movement per breath for much of the class.
  • Sequences vary from class to class. The teacher chooses the order, which is what separates it from Ashtanga.
  • Often labeled Flow, Power Flow, Slow Flow or Vinyasa Flow. Slow Flow means longer holds and fewer transitions.

What an hour looks like

  • A short warm-up, then repeated rounds of linked movement that build in length.
  • Standing sequences in the middle third, often the most demanding part.
  • Floor shapes and rest at the end, usually shorter than in a Hatha class.

Things to know before your first one

  • The pace assumes you already know the shapes. Two or three Hatha classes first makes a large difference.
  • You are allowed to take Child's Pose at any point without asking. Everyone does.
  • Wrists and shoulders take repeated load. If they are new to it, take the knees down in the plank-like transitions.

Closest sequence here

  • The standing section of the Gentle Full Practice, run without the pauses between shapes.

Yin

Yin, from the Chinese concept of yin and yang

What it is

  • Floor-based shapes held for two to five minutes each, with the muscles deliberately relaxed rather than engaged.
  • A class covers perhaps six to eight shapes in an hour, which is very few compared with any other style.
  • Props are used generously so the body can be still without holding itself up.

What a long hold feels like

  • The first thirty seconds feel like an ordinary stretch.
  • Between one and two minutes, most people meet a point where the mind starts negotiating. This is the part the practice is really about.
  • Coming out slowly matters. Move gradually and expect the area to feel briefly vulnerable for a minute afterward.

What to watch

  • Long passive holds should be a broad, tolerable sensation. Sharp, electrical or joint-line sensation is a signal to come out and add support.
  • Yin is often described as easy because it looks still. It is not, and it is the style most likely to be overdone by people who are naturally flexible.

Closest sequence here

  • Evening Wind-Down, which uses the same long supported holds.

Restorative

An English term, no traditional Sanskrit equivalent

What it is

  • Four to six fully supported shapes over an hour, each held for five to twenty minutes.
  • Props do all the work. Bolsters, blankets, blocks, straps, eye covers and sometimes sandbags.
  • Unlike Yin, there is no intentional stretch sensation. If you feel a stretch, the setup needs more support.

What an hour looks like

  • Ten minutes of setting up the first shape, which is normal and not wasted time.
  • Long stillness with the teacher adjusting props and speaking very little.
  • Very slow transitions between shapes.

Who tends to like it

  • People who find sitting still difficult and want the props to make it possible.
  • Anyone practicing in the evening.
  • People who are managing fatigue and want a practice that does not add load.

Closest sequence here

  • Evening Wind-Down run at double the hold times, with more blankets.

Iyengar

Named after B. K. S. Iyengar, 1918 to 2014

What it is

  • Precise attention to alignment in each pose, with long holds and detailed verbal instruction.
  • Extensive use of props, and Iyengar teaching is where most of the prop vocabulary used across modern yoga originated.
  • Teachers complete a long, structured training and assessment process, which is unusually rigorous compared with the wider industry.

What an hour looks like

  • Fewer poses than a Hatha class, each one built in stages and often repeated two or three times.
  • Time spent standing still while the teacher explains a specific action.
  • Props issued at the start and used constantly.

Who tends to like it

  • People who want to understand exactly what each pose is asking of them.
  • Anyone who has found other classes too fast to follow safely.
  • People working around a specific limitation who want props used well.

Closest sequence here

  • Any of our pose guides practiced one at a time with the props set up as written.

Ashtanga

Aṣṭāṅga, meaning eight limbs

What it is

  • A fixed sequence of poses practiced in the same order every time, with a set breathing pattern and specific transitions.
  • The Primary Series is the first of several. Most practitioners spend years on it.
  • Often taught Mysore style, where everyone in the room works through their own portion of the sequence at their own pace while the teacher moves between students.

What to expect

  • A led class runs 75 to 90 minutes and is physically demanding throughout.
  • A Mysore class is the more approachable entry point despite sounding like the advanced option, because you are only doing the portion you have been given.
  • Repetition is the method. The point is doing the same thing many times and noticing what changes.

Things to consider

  • The fixed order means the sequence does not adapt to you, so you adapt within it. A good teacher will modify individual poses.
  • The volume of repeated shoulder and wrist loading is significant. Build up slowly.

Closest sequence here

  • Nothing on this site is close in intensity. The 10-Minute Morning shares the idea of repeating the same practice daily.

Reading a class description

Common timetable words and what they usually signal
Words on the scheduleWhat it usually means
All levelsModifications will be offered. It does not mean slow.
Level 1, Foundations, BasicsGenuinely aimed at people in their first year. The best place to start.
Level 2 or 3, IntermediateAssumes you know the shape names and can hold them without instruction.
Power, Sculpt, FitnessFast, strength focused, usually little rest.
Slow FlowVinyasa with longer holds and fewer transitions. A good bridge from Hatha.
GentleSlower pace, more floor work, more props. Not the same as Restorative.
Hot, 95 degrees, heatedRoom temperature well above normal. Approach carefully as a beginner.
MysoreSelf-paced Ashtanga with individual attention. Beginners are welcome despite the name.
Chair yogaEntire practice in and around a chair. A real practice, not a reduced one.
PrenatalSpecifically designed for pregnancy by a teacher with additional training.

Whatever the label says, arriving five minutes early and telling the teacher it is your first class, and mentioning anything they should know about your body, changes the hour more than the style name does.

How to find a teacher near you

The label matters less than showing up and saying so

Arriving five minutes early, naming it your first class, and mentioning anything the teacher should know about your body changes the hour more than any word on the schedule.
