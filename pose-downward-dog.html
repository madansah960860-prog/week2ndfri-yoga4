Pose guide

Downward-Facing Dog

Adho Mukha Śvānāsana

An inverted V shape that loads the shoulders and wrists while lengthening the muscles along the back of the legs and the spine. Widely taught, widely rushed.

Beginner with modifications Hold 30 to 60 seconds Shoulders, hamstrings, calves, spine Blocks or wall

Pose breakdown

Adho meaning downward, mukha meaning face, śvāna meaning dog

Entry steps

  1. Start on hands and knees with the hands directly under the shoulders and the knees under the hips.
  2. Spread the fingers wide and point the middle fingers straight forward. Press down through the base of the index finger and the thumb, not just the outer edge of the hand.
  3. Walk the hands forward about the length of one hand so there is room for the torso to lengthen.
  4. Tuck the toes under.
  5. Press the floor away and lift the hips up and back with the knees clearly bent. Do not straighten the legs yet.
  6. Reach the sitting bones toward the back of the ceiling until you feel the spine lengthen from the wrists to the hips.
  7. Only now begin to send the heels toward the floor, keeping as much knee bend as the spine needs to stay long. The heels may never touch, and that is not a problem.

Alignment cues

  • The spine sets the shape, not the heels. A long spine with bent knees is the pose. A rounded spine with straight legs is a different and less useful shape.
  • Outward spiral in the upper arms. Turn the eye of each elbow slightly toward the thumb so the shoulders are not jammed up around the ears.
  • Ears between the arms. Let the head hang without dropping the neck into a hard crease, and without lifting it to look forward.
  • Even weight through the hands. Most wrist discomfort comes from all the load sitting in the heel of the palm. Actively grip the mat with the fingertips.
  • Feet hip width and parallel. Turning the toes out slackens the hips and makes the shape harder to hold.
  • Ribs in line. Draw the front ribs gently in so the low back does not sag toward the floor.

Breath pattern

  • Nose breathing throughout, at an even pace of roughly four counts in and four to five out.
  • Do not synchronize with movement here. Once you are in the shape, the breath is steady rather than driving anything.
  • If the breath becomes short or you notice yourself holding it, the shape has become too demanding. Bend the knees more or lower to the floor.

Suggested hold

  • In a moving sequence: 3 to 5 breaths, roughly 20 to 30 seconds.
  • As a held shape: 30 to 60 seconds, repeated two or three times with a rest in Child's Pose between.
  • Building wrist and shoulder tolerance: three rounds of 20 seconds is better than one round of 60.

Common mistakes

  • Straightening the legs first. This rounds the low back and removes most of what the pose is doing. Bend the knees and lift the hips first, every time.
  • Hands too close to the feet. A short stance stacks weight into the shoulders and wrists. Walk the hands further forward.
  • Chasing the heels to the floor. Heel height is determined by calf and hamstring length and by ankle structure. It is not a measure of progress.
  • Collapsing between the shoulder blades. If the chest sinks toward the floor, press the ground away more firmly.
  • Holding it too long, too early. This is a full body load. Ninety seconds on day one is how people end up with sore wrists on day two.

Accessible modifications

  • Wall Dog. Stand facing a wall, place the hands on it at hip height, and walk the feet back until the arms and torso form one line roughly parallel to the floor. This is the same shoulder and spine action with none of the wrist load, and it is the version to learn the pose in.
  • Chair Dog. Place the hands on the seat of a sturdy chair set against a wall so it cannot slide. Walk the feet back and lengthen the spine. A useful middle step between the wall and the floor.
  • Blocks under the hands. Two blocks on their tallest setting raise the floor toward you, which lets the spine lengthen when hamstrings are limiting the shape.
  • Forearm version, Dolphin. Come down onto the forearms with the elbows shoulder width. This removes the wrists from the equation entirely and asks more of the shoulders.
  • Blanket roll under the heels. Roll a blanket and place it under the heels so they have contact with something. This settles the nervous fidgeting that comes from floating heels.
  • Knees down. Simply keeping the knees bent at 45 degrees for the whole hold is a complete, legitimate version of this pose.

Progression

  • Three-legged Dog. From a steady Down Dog, lift one leg no higher than the line of the spine, keeping both hips level. Hold 15 to 20 seconds per side. Lifting the leg higher by opening the hip is a different pose and usually an unintentional one.
  • Dog to Plank transitions. Shift forward into a plank on an inhale, press back on an exhale, five to eight times. This builds the shoulder endurance that makes longer holds available.
Approach with care Take extra care or choose the wall or chair version if you have a wrist, elbow or shoulder injury, carpal tunnel syndrome, uncontrolled high blood pressure, glaucoma or any condition where a healthcare professional has advised against putting the head below the heart, if you are in later pregnancy, or if you have had recent abdominal or shoulder surgery. Stop if you feel sharp wrist pain, shoulder pinching, numbness or tingling in the hands, or pressure in the head. Work with a qualified teacher for hands-on guidance. A note on learning this one

Downward-Facing Dog is usually the third or fourth pose a beginner meets, and it is far more demanding than its position in most class sequences suggests. It asks the shoulders to hold body weight overhead, the wrists to bear load at an extended angle, and the backs of the legs to lengthen, all at once.

Spend two weeks in Wall Dog before you spend any time in the floor version. Nothing is lost by the delay, and the shape you arrive at will be a better one.

It appears in the 10-Minute Morning and the Gentle Full Practice, with the wall option written into both.

What to feel for

Length first, heels second

The signal you want is a long line from wrist to hip with the knees still bent, not straight legs with a rounded back. Weight should sit evenly across the whole hand, not just the heel of the palm. If the breath goes short or the shoulders start climbing toward the ears, that is the shape asking for less — bend the knees more or come down to Wall Dog for the rest of the hold.
