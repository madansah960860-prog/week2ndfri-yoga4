Practice at home

Choosing a spot, measuring the floor, dealing with cold, noise and hard surfaces, and building something that survives an ordinary bad week.

Updated 08/25/2026 · About 13 minutes to read

How much space you actually need

A standard mat is 68 inches long and 24 inches wide, so about 5 feet 8 inches by 2 feet. That is the minimum footprint. To move properly you want clearance around it.

Floor practice only

Seated, kneeling and lying shapes: about 6 feet by 3 feet.

Full practice with standing poses

About 7 feet by 4 feet, so the arms can open to the sides without hitting anything.

Chair practice

About 3 feet by 3 feet around the chair.

Wall poses

A clear stretch of wall about 3 feet wide, plus 6 feet of floor out from it.

The quick test: lie on your back and open your arms out to the sides. If nothing hits a wall, a table leg or a radiator, you have enough. Do this test in the actual spot before you commit to it, because rooms are consistently smaller than they look.

Choosing the spot

The best spot is the one with the fewest steps between deciding to practice and starting. Every obstacle between those two points is a place where the practice gets abandoned.

  • Can you leave the mat out? If yes, that spot wins almost regardless of any other factor. Rolling a mat out is a small task, and small tasks are where habits die.
  • Is anything in the way? A coffee table you have to move each time costs you thirty seconds and some willingness.
  • Is there a wall? A clear wall makes a large number of poses available.
  • Who else uses the room? Practicing in a shared space at a time when someone will walk through changes what you are willing to do.
  • What is the light like at the time you will practice? Harsh overhead lighting reads badly at any hour. A softer source off to one side is more comfortable whether it is seven in the morning or nine at night.

Floors

Hardwood or laminate

Good for standing poses, hard on knees. Always have a blanket for kneeling shapes. Check for slipperiness in socks before you trust it.

Tile or concrete

Cold and unforgiving. Use a mat plus a blanket, and expect to want socks.

Low pile carpet

Comfortable for floor work. Put a mat on top for standing poses, because feet sink into carpet and the base becomes unstable.

Thick carpet or rug over pad

Fine for everything except balance poses, where you want something firmer underfoot.

An uneven or sloping floor

Orient the mat so any slope runs side to side rather than head to foot, and avoid balance poses on the slope.

Light

Daylight is the easiest light to practice in and needs nothing from you beyond facing away from a bright window rather than into it. Once the sun is down, the thing to manage is overhead lighting: a ceiling fixture flattens everything and is uncomfortable when you are lying on your back looking up at it.

  • Turn the overhead off and use a softer source off to one side instead, out of your eyeline rather than pointed at your face.
  • Warm bulbs, the ones labeled soft white or warm white, are gentler on the eyes than a cool daylight bulb at close range.
  • For morning practice, natural light is enough on its own.

Temperature

Body temperature drops noticeably once you stop moving, which is why the last ten minutes of a long practice is when people get cold. Around 68 to 72 degrees Fahrenheit is comfortable for active practice. For a supported evening practice with long stillness, aim a little warmer, or plan to cover up.

  • Have a blanket or a sweater within arm's reach before you start, not five minutes into the final rest.
  • Socks are fine in floor and supported practice. Take them off for standing work unless they have grip.
  • Avoid practicing directly under an air vent or in front of a fan.

Noise and interruption

  • Phone. Face down and out of arm's reach, or in another room. If you are using our sequence player, put the device on the floor beside the mat with notifications silenced.
  • Household noise. Practicing with noise in the background is a skill and it is worth building rather than waiting for silence that never arrives.
  • Other people. Tell them what you are doing and how long it will take. Twenty minutes is easier to protect when it has been announced.
  • Pets. A cat will get on your mat. This is not solvable. Shut the door or accept it.

What to keep in the space

  • Mat, two blocks, a strap, two blankets. That is the working kit.
  • A bolster or two firm pillows if you practice in the evening.
  • A chair, if you use one. Keep it in the room rather than carrying it in.
  • A printed copy of whichever sequence you are working on, which is what our print stylesheet is for.

Store props somewhere visible. Props in a closet are props you will not use. A basket in the corner of the room is enough.

Building a routine that survives

Most home practices fail in week three, when the novelty has worn off and nothing has replaced it. Here is what tends to work.

  • Attach it to something that already happens. After brushing your teeth. Before the shower. After the kids are down. An existing anchor beats a time on a clock.
  • Pick a length you can do on your worst day, not your best. If ten minutes is the honest number, make ten minutes the plan. Doing more on a good day is easy. Doing less on a bad day feels like failing.
  • Repeat one sequence. Novelty is the enemy of noticing. The fourth time through the 10-Minute Morning is when you start feeling the difference between your two hips.
  • Have a two minute version. On the days when the full thing is not going to happen, do Child's Pose and Legs Up the Wall. It keeps the thread unbroken.
  • Track it in the simplest possible way. A mark on a paper calendar. Nothing that requires opening an app.
  • Expect gaps. Missing three days is not a reset. Start again on day four exactly where you left off.

A first week, written out

  • Day 1. Set up the space. Measure the floor. Do 10-Minute Morning.
  • Day 2. Rest, or two minutes of Legs Up the Wall.
  • Day 3. 10-Minute Morning again. Notice what is different from day one.
  • Day 4. Desk Reset, at whatever point in the day your shoulders start climbing.
  • Day 5. Rest.
  • Day 6. 10-Minute Morning.
  • Day 7. Evening Wind-Down, whenever the evening empties out.

That is about fifty five minutes across a week. Repeat it for three more weeks before you change anything.
